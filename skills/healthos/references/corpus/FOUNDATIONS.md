# HealthOS foundations

This is a compact foundation course for making sense of personal health evidence. It teaches a way to reason, not a universal treatment plan. Each lesson can stand alone. A teacher should present one bounded section, connect it to the learner's goal, and check understanding before moving on.

## HOS-FND-01 — Define health in the person's own terms

Health data becomes useful when it serves a decision or a life the person values. Begin with three horizons:

- **Now:** symptoms, safety, function, treatment burden, and quality of life.
- **Next:** risks or capacities likely to matter over the coming years.
- **Later:** preserving independence, cognition, relationships, and the ability to do meaningful things.

The horizons interact. A long-term risk reduction that creates intolerable present harm is not automatically a good trade. Nor should a normal day-to-day feeling erase a well-supported silent risk. The job is to make tradeoffs visible.

Useful questions include: What do you want to remain able to do? What outcome worries you? What burden are you willing to accept? What would make a change worthwhile? These are not laboratory values, but they determine which values matter.

**Checkpoint:** Name one outcome that matters now and one capability you want to preserve later. What evidence would help with each?

## HOS-FND-02 — Separate evidence, guidance, interpretation, and action

A personal record contains different kinds of truth:

- A lab report is evidence that a method produced a value under particular conditions.
- A guideline is advice for a defined population, jurisdiction, and decision.
- An interpretation connects evidence and guidance, sometimes with uncertainty.
- An action is a choice made by the person or a qualified decision-maker.

Problems arise when these are collapsed. “The report flags this value” is not the same as “you have a disease.” “An expert prefers this target” is not the same as “current local guidance recommends treatment for you.” “We discussed exercise” is not the same as “you started the plan.”

Good HealthOS answers show the transitions. That makes them auditable and easier to correct.

**Checkpoint:** Classify each statement: “LDL-C was reported as X,” “this pattern may increase risk,” and “I will discuss medication at my appointment.”

## HOS-FND-03 — Understand a measurement before comparing it

Every measurement has a definition, method, unit, time, and context. Some are directly measured; others are calculated or estimated. Some fluctuate minute to minute; others summarize weeks or months. A device metric may be useful for self-comparison without being interchangeable with a clinical test.

Four numeric boundaries are often confused:

1. a laboratory reference interval describing a comparison population;
2. a diagnostic threshold used with specified conditions;
3. a decision threshold where benefits and harms of action may change; and
4. a personalized target selected for a particular goal.

These numbers can differ without contradiction. Always attach the unit and source. Before calling a change real, ask whether the assay, device, specimen, timing, health state, and preparation were comparable and whether ordinary biological or measurement variation could explain it.

**Checkpoint:** Why can two correct sources give different “normal” or target numbers for the same marker?

## HOS-FND-04 — Build a cardiometabolic risk picture

Cardiometabolic health is not one score or lab. A useful picture connects blood pressure, atherogenic lipoprotein exposure, glucose regulation, kidney function, tobacco or nicotine exposure, activity, sleep, body composition, medications, family history, and prior disease. The relative importance changes by person and time horizon.

Common lipid measures answer different questions. Total cholesterol is a broad sum. LDL-C and non-HDL-C describe cholesterol carried in selected particle groups. Triglycerides reflect another lipid pool and can vary with recent conditions. Apolipoprotein B estimates the number of atherogenic particles more directly than cholesterol mass does. Lipoprotein(a) is a partly inherited risk marker whose assay and unit need to be preserved exactly.

Glucose measures also have different time windows. A single glucose value depends on timing and context. Hemoglobin A1c reflects a longer period but can be distorted when red-cell biology differs. Continuous or home measurements provide patterns but introduce device and sampling limitations.

Risk is cumulative, but personal decisions still depend on absolute risk, prior events, age, comorbidities, treatment benefit, adverse effects, and preferences. A metric should not be optimized in isolation.

**Checkpoint:** If a lipid value improves after a medication change, what context and additional evidence would you want before attributing the change and deciding the next step?

## HOS-FND-05 — Reason about prevention and screening

Prevention includes reducing exposures, vaccination, managing established risks, recognizing symptoms promptly, and using screening when evidence supports it. Screening tests look for disease or precursors in people without relevant symptoms. Symptoms require evaluation, not a screening calendar.

A screening recommendation is never just a test name. It specifies a target population, starting and stopping conditions, interval, method, and follow-up pathway. Age, anatomy, pregnancy, family history, genetics, prior findings, exposures, life expectancy, and local program quality may change it.

Screening can help, but it can also create false positives, false negatives, overdiagnosis, invasive follow-up, anxiety, and treatment burden. Its value depends on whether earlier detection improves outcomes in that population and whether high-quality follow-up is available.

The chance that a result is a true signal depends partly on how likely the condition was before testing and on the test's performance in that setting. A test that performs well can still produce many false positives when used in a very-low-risk population. This is why a result's positive or negative predictive value cannot be carried unchanged from one population to another.

Maintain a screening view with separate fields for eligibility, last completed test, result, recommended next review, source guideline, and unresolved risk modifiers. Do not mark “not documented” as “never done.” A screening pathway is not closed merely because a test was ordered or performed: record whether the result was received, communicated, and followed through.

**Checkpoint:** What is the first distinction to make when someone with a new symptom asks whether they are due for screening?

## HOS-FND-06 — Include brain, sleep, and emotional health

Cognition, mood, sleep, sensory function, and social context belong in the same longitudinal system as labs and medications. They are experienced outcomes as well as influences on other behaviors and risks.

Sleep should be described by opportunity, duration, regularity, timing, continuity, symptoms, and daytime function rather than by one wearable score. Snoring, witnessed breathing pauses, severe daytime sleepiness, insomnia, unusual movements, and medication or substance effects may warrant clinical evaluation. A consumer device can identify patterns but cannot by itself rule a sleep disorder in or out.

For cognition or mood, establish baseline function and change over time. Acute confusion, abrupt neurological change, suicidal intent, or inability to remain safe requires urgent local help. For non-urgent concerns, record onset, course, functional impact, medications, sleep, substances, sensory changes, stressors, and professional assessments without reducing the person to a score.

**Checkpoint:** Why is “sleep score 82” weaker evidence than a timeline combining device data, sleep opportunity, symptoms, and daytime function?

## HOS-FND-07 — Treat physical capacity as several trainable qualities

Physical capacity has multiple components:

- habitual movement and sedentary exposure;
- aerobic endurance and cardiorespiratory fitness;
- maximal strength and muscular endurance;
- power, or producing force quickly;
- balance, coordination, and agility;
- mobility and task-specific range of motion; and
- recovery, pain, and tolerance.

No single metric represents all of them. A step count measures movement exposure, not fitness. A race time blends fitness, skill, terrain, and conditions. A strength test is specific to its movement and technique. Wearable recovery scores are estimates, not diagnoses.

Choose measurements that match the person's future tasks and current safety. Standardize the protocol before interpreting change. Training should account for medical status, injury, accessibility, and progression; current public guidance provides a baseline, while individual programming may require a qualified professional.

**Checkpoint:** Give one measure of exposure, one of capacity, and one of function. Why should they not be merged into one score?

## HOS-FND-08 — Evaluate nutrition by pattern, adequacy, response, and sustainability

Nutrition is more than a named diet. A useful review asks:

- **Pattern:** What foods and beverages make up ordinary weeks?
- **Adequacy:** Are energy, protein, essential fats, fibre, vitamins, minerals, and fluids sufficient for the person and goal?
- **Balance and response:** How do intake, activity, appetite, symptoms, body composition, labs, and medications interact?
- **Sustainability:** Does the approach fit culture, enjoyment, cost, access, skills, and social life?

Food records are estimates and can change behavior while being recorded. Weight is an outcome influenced by many factors, not a moral score. Biomarkers may help test a specific nutritional hypothesis but rarely reveal the complete diet.

Prefer an explicit goal over ideology: improving glycaemic control, supporting training, correcting a deficiency, reducing cardiovascular risk, managing a symptom with clinical support, or making meals easier. Judge an approach by outcomes, burden, safety, and adherence context. Eating disorders, significant unexplained weight change, pregnancy, kidney disease, and other clinical situations require specialized care.

**Checkpoint:** What evidence would distinguish “this diet is healthy in general” from “this pattern is working safely for this person and goal”?

## HOS-FND-09 — Reconcile medications and supplements as interventions

A medication list is only useful when it reflects reality. For each product, capture the exact name, formulation, dose, route, schedule, indication, prescriber or decision owner, start and stop dates, adherence context, effects, adverse effects, interactions, and monitoring plan when known.

Separate “listed in a record,” “prescribed,” “dispensed,” and “actually taking.” Preserve uncertainty. A discontinued item remains historically relevant.

Supplements can have physiologic effects, interactions, contaminants, variable formulations, and laboratory effects. Regulatory review differs by country and product category. “Natural” does not establish safety or benefit. Evaluate the exact product and dose, not only the ingredient name, and involve a clinician or pharmacist where risk or interaction is plausible.

**Checkpoint:** Why is a product name alone insufficient for medication reconciliation?

## HOS-FND-10 — Run measured actions without fooling yourself

An action becomes learnable when its goal, baseline, intervention, measure, review point, and stop conditions are explicit. Use the lowest-burden design that can answer the question safely.

Changing many things at once can still improve life, but it makes attribution difficult. Short-term proxy improvements may not represent long-term benefit. Regression to the mean, expectation, season, illness, adherence, and unrelated treatment changes can all alter results.

Predefine what would count as success, no meaningful change, unacceptable burden, or harm. Record what actually happened rather than only the plan. At review, decide whether to continue, adapt, stop, seek clinical input, or collect better evidence.

**Checkpoint:** Before starting a behavior experiment, which decision rule would prevent you from declaring success merely because you hoped it would work?

## HOS-FND-11 — Maintain a useful longitudinal view

A good current view is concise because the underlying history is preserved elsewhere. It should show:

- the person's goals and decision context;
- active conditions, symptoms, treatments, and relevant allergies;
- material risk and protective factors;
- important recent measurements with dates and trends only where justified;
- prevention and screening status with unknowns;
- active actions and review points;
- unanswered questions and data-quality limits; and
- links back to exact evidence.

Rebuild this view when evidence changes. Do not copy old uncertainty forward as fact. A sparse honest view is safer than a complete-looking one filled with assumptions.

**Checkpoint:** Which parts of a HealthOS should be frozen, appended, and freely rebuilt—and why?

## HOS-FND-12 — Build a qualitative prevention-priority review

A broad preventive review is a dated map of what deserves attention, not a diagnosis, prediction, or single longevity score. Start with immediate safety, then consider major disease pathways, physical and cognitive reserve, emotional and social health, avoidable exposures, and whether ordinary care tasks reliably reach completion.

For each domain, separate risk factors, protective factors, evidence gaps, and next decision. Use explicit coverage states such as `known`, `partial`, `pending`, `unknown`, and `not applicable`. Unknown is not low risk; it is a reason to decide whether better evidence would change anything.

Rank work by consequence, plausible personal relevance, evidence that an action helps, urgency, reversibility, burden, and confidence in the underlying data. Keep these ingredients visible instead of compressing them into a fake probability. A prevention priority may justify learning, collecting evidence, or discussing care; it cannot activate a treatment by itself.

**Checkpoint:** Why might a high-consequence unknown rank above a well-measured but modest risk—and what would you do next without pretending to know the person's probability?
