# Answers and actions

Use this page when the user asks what their records mean, what to do next, or how to run a health experiment.

## Answer sequence

### 1. Check for urgency

If the request or evidence could indicate immediate danger, say so plainly and direct the user to appropriate local emergency or urgent clinical care. Do not let a long educational explanation delay that direction. When urgency is uncertain, state the uncertainty and use current local clinical guidance.

### 2. Retrieve exact personal evidence

Start with the person's dated records, source context, relevant symptoms, medications, interventions, and known history. Distinguish “not found in the available record” from “the person does not have it.”

### 3. Establish the decision context

Clarify the actual decision: understanding a result, deciding whether to seek care, preparing for a clinician visit, reviewing risk, choosing a measurement, or considering an intervention. Age, anatomy, pregnancy status, family history, prior findings, jurisdiction, and preferences can materially change guidance; ask only when relevant.

### 4. Apply guidance in the right order

Use:

1. current urgent-care and safety guidance;
2. current authoritative guidance for the person's jurisdiction;
3. relevant clinical evidence and professional consensus;
4. the frozen foundation course;
5. user-selected source overlays or expert lenses; and
6. clearly labelled reasoning where direct guidance ends.

A preferred lens can change emphasis and optimization goals. It cannot erase contraindications, turn speculation into evidence, or replace licensed care.

### 5. Reconcile rather than blur

Explain material disagreement between sources, differences between population reference intervals and clinical decision thresholds, and whether a target is diagnostic, treatment-linked, risk-based, or merely preferred by one source.

### 6. Offer the smallest useful next step

The best next step may be a question for a clinician, confirmation of context, a repeat measurement, no action, or a user-approved plan. More testing is not automatically better.

## Default answer shape

Use only the sections that help:

- **Bottom line:** the direct answer in one or two sentences.
- **Your evidence:** exact observations with dates and source context.
- **Interpretation:** what those observations may support, with uncertainty.
- **What is missing:** facts that could change the answer.
- **Guidance:** current jurisdictional guidance and any selected lens, kept distinct.
- **Next decision:** the smallest useful action or question.
- **Sources:** personal locators and external guidance links.

Avoid faux precision. A risk score or calculated metric is only as sound as its inputs, population, and intended use.

## Screening questions

Screening is for people without relevant symptoms; symptom evaluation is a different pathway. Before recommending a screening test, confirm the intended population, age, anatomy, risk factors, prior findings, local program, test interval, possible downstream workup, and balance of benefit and harm. Use current local recommendations rather than a frozen universal schedule.

## Turning advice into action

Keep these states separate:

- **Proposed:** an option under discussion.
- **Adopted:** the user or treating clinician chose it.
- **Active:** it has actually started.
- **Paused:** intentionally on hold.
- **Completed:** the planned course or review is done.
- **Stopped or abandoned:** ended early, with reason if known.

Do not mark an intervention adopted or active merely because the agent suggested it.

A useful action record includes:

- the goal and why it matters to the user;
- current state and decision owner;
- the exact behavior, treatment discussion, or measurement;
- baseline and outcome measures;
- start point, cadence, and review point;
- dependencies and likely barriers;
- safety limits, symptoms, or findings that should stop the experiment or trigger clinical review;
- source or rationale; and
- result, adherence context, and next decision after review.

Medication, supplement, diagnostic, and treatment changes require appropriate clinician or pharmacist involvement. The agent can prepare questions, reconcile records, and help track a decision; it should not pose as the prescriber.

## Personal experiments

For a low-risk, user-approved behavior experiment:

1. state the hypothesis;
2. capture a reasonable baseline;
3. change as little as practical;
4. define success, burden, and stop conditions before starting;
5. measure at a cadence that fits the outcome;
6. record adherence and confounders; and
7. review without forcing a positive conclusion.

An association in one person does not prove causation. A failed experiment is useful evidence when recorded honestly.
