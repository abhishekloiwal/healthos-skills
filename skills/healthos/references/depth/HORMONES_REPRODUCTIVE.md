## HOS-DEP-HR-01 — Thyroid tests have different jobs

**Purpose:** Explain why symptoms, TSH, free hormone, and treatment response cannot substitute for one another.

The thyroid supplies mostly T4, which tissues can convert to the more active T3 through deiodinase enzymes. TSH is part of the feedback system asking the thyroid to produce hormone. It is a signal about regulation, rather than the substance directly responsible for every associated symptom. Binding proteins add another distinction: total hormone includes the bound fraction, whereas free hormone measures a different pool.

In ordinary primary thyroid failure, clinicians interpret TSH together with free T4. Antibodies can indicate an autoimmune process without establishing current hormone deficiency. Fatigue, weight change, hair loss, or brain fog are also insufficient on their own because other conditions can produce them. Fasting can alter T3 production and clearance as part of physiological adaptation, rather than proving permanent thyroid disease.

Assessing established replacement treatment raises a related but different question: has the person's health and function improved? Persistent symptoms deserve attention to other explanations, treatment use, measurements, and clinical context. They do not automatically establish a need for more hormone. Conversely, a normalized feedback marker does not explain away every remaining complaint.

Unexpected results may involve timing, binding, or assay interference, including some supplement effects. Investigating a mismatch is more informative than repeatedly adjusting treatment to chase one number. The source's discussion of combination therapy is useful for understanding this question, but observational differences between treated groups do not establish that adding T3 causes better survival.

## HOS-DEP-HR-02 — Menopause care depends on symptoms, route, and purpose

**Purpose:** Distinguish hormone fluctuation, local tissue treatment, systemic treatment, and contraception.

Perimenopause often involves variable cycles and hormone swings rather than a steady decline. A single sample captures one moment. A timeline of bleeding, sleep, vasomotor symptoms, mood, sexual comfort, and medication changes can show patterns that the sample misses. The timeline supports assessment without assuming every midlife symptom comes from ovarian aging.

Treatment names can hide different purposes. Systemic menopausal hormone therapy affects the body more broadly; low-dose local treatment addresses genitourinary tissue symptoms. Vaginal delivery alone does not establish which category a product belongs to: some vaginal products deliver systemic exposure. Likewise, oral and transdermal routes have different absorption and liver effects, so equal-looking labels are not enough to compare them.

Uterine status matters when systemic estrogen is considered because endometrial protection is a separate treatment requirement. Feeling better or sleeping well does not demonstrate that the endometrium is adequately protected. Pregnancy prevention is another distinct question: usual menopausal hormone therapy is not reliable contraception.

Genitourinary concerns can include urinary symptoms and painful sex as well as dryness, and they may persist during systemic treatment. They deserve their own assessment rather than automatic systemic dose escalation. A useful consultation identifies the symptom being treated, exact product and route, relevant medical history, contraception needs, tolerability, and intended review. Claims about long-term prevention need evidence for that outcome; symptom relief alone does not demonstrate dementia prevention or establish safety across cancer histories.

## HOS-DEP-HR-03 — Fertility measures describe stages, not guarantees

**Purpose:** Interpret reproductive tests and success rates without collapsing the path to a live birth into one number.

Conception depends on several processes: sperm production and maturation, ovulation, egg quality, transport, fertilization, embryo development, and implantation. Both partners' circumstances and time horizons matter. A reassuring result at one stage cannot establish that every other stage works.

A semen analysis describes a pattern of volume, concentration, movement, and morphology. Biological and collection variability make a single result incomplete. Shape is also different from chromosome status: a low percentage meeting strict morphology criteria does not mean the remaining sperm carry genetic disease. Hormone measurements answer another question. Exogenous testosterone can raise circulating testosterone while suppressing signals needed for sperm production, illustrating why improving one measurement can conflict with a reproductive goal.

For ovarian assessment, AMH is useful for anticipating stimulation response and egg yield. It does not directly measure chromosomal quality or reliably predict spontaneous conception on its own. Age and the broader reproductive assessment remain relevant.

Treatment statistics need equally careful reading. In Amato's simplified teaching example, ten retrieved eggs might lead to seven fertilized eggs and three or four blastocysts. These are illustrative stages from the discussion, not a forecast. An implantation percentage for a selected embryo is conditional on reaching that stage; it cannot be advertised as success per retrieval or treatment started. Compare clinics or options using the same endpoint, denominator, patient population, and follow-up. Preservation of eggs or sperm creates a future option, with further attrition and uncertainty still ahead.

## HOS-DEP-HR-04 — Sexual health begins with the person's experience

**Purpose:** Separate desire, arousal, comfort, and satisfaction while preserving choice and avoiding unnecessary medicalization.

Sexual health has several dimensions. Someone may want intimacy but experience pain, have interest without sufficient arousal, or enjoy sexual activity without making orgasm the goal. A frequency target cannot describe these differences. Begin with what the person wants to change, if anything, and whether the concern troubles them rather than only a partner.

Desire can be spontaneous or develop within a welcome, comfortable context. Responsive desire helps explain why immediate interest is not everyone's starting point. It never makes unwanted contact appropriate: willingness to explore is voluntary, and the person can decline or stop without needing to justify the decision. Planned time together is an opportunity, not an obligation to have sex.

Comfort also has several contributors. Lubrication varies with life stage, medication and other circumstances; it is not a reliable test of attraction. Friction, tissue changes, and pelvic-floor tension can contribute to a cycle of pain and guarding. A tense pelvic floor may need relaxation and coordination rather than generic strengthening advice. Persistent pain warrants assessment instead of pressure to continue through it.

Sleep, stress, relationship context, past experiences, medications, and reproductive stage can all inform the conversation. This broader view helps distinguish a need for information, communication, tissue care, professional support, or investigation. Hormones and desire medications address selected problems; they do not replace understanding the person's experience or establish one normal sexual response.
