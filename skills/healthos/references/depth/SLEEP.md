# Understanding sleep problems

This route explains why different sleep complaints need different questions. It builds from sleep regulation and measurement to persistent insomnia and the interpretation of treatment outcomes. It is educational preparation for understanding care, not a personal treatment schedule.

**Prerequisites:** `HOS-FND-03` — measurements and context; `HOS-FND-06` — sleep, symptoms, and daytime function. For the final unit, also review `HOS-FND-09` — medications and supplements as interventions.

**Unit map:**

## HOS-DEP-SL-01 — Separate the sleep clock from sleep pressure

**Purpose:** Explain why sleepiness, available time in bed, and the ability to sleep do not always coincide.

Two interacting processes help organize sleep. Sleep pressure increases across time awake and falls during sleep. Circadian timing produces a daily rhythm of greater wakefulness and greater readiness for sleep. A long period awake increases pressure without instantly moving the clock. Conversely, a nap can reduce pressure without shifting the clock to a new schedule. Caffeine can mask the experience of pressure; feeling more alert after it does not demonstrate that the need for sleep disappeared.

These distinctions make the same complaint more informative. Difficulty sleeping at an unusually early bedtime could involve timing, while difficulty after substantial daytime sleep could involve reduced pressure. Neither example supplies a diagnosis. Opportunity matters separately: a person whose commitments leave little time for sleep faces a different problem from someone who has adequate opportunity but remains awake and distressed.

A useful description therefore combines usual timing, opportunity, estimated sleep, awakenings, and daytime function across several days. Each measure answers a different question. A diary captures recalled experience; a wearable estimates patterns through its sensors and algorithms. Even an objective light sensor can measure the wrist's environment rather than the light reaching the eyes. Agreement between measurements adds context, while disagreement invites examination of methods and experience before assigning a disorder or optimizing a score.

**Source basis:** [D341](SOURCES.md#d341), E341-30 [01:46:51] and E341-17 [01:06:27]; [D394](SOURCES.md#d394), E394-02 [00:03:04]; [D286](SOURCES.md#d286), E286-06 [00:28:07].

**Check:** Two people report six hours of sleep. One allows only six hours in bed; the other allows nine hours but struggles to sleep. What information does the shared number hide?

**Expected answer:** Sleep opportunity, timing, continuity, distress, and daytime effects differ. Duration alone cannot identify the mechanism or establish insomnia; device and diary methods also need context.

## HOS-DEP-SL-02 — Distinguish what started insomnia from what maintains it

**Purpose:** Understand why persistent insomnia may require more than removing an original stressor or improving the bedroom.

A disruption can begin with illness, loss, work demands, or another stressful event. What keeps it going may change. Someone who initially lost sleep during a crisis might later spend much longer in bed trying to recover, become watchful for signs of wakefulness, or anticipate failure the next day. The original threat can recede while these responses continue. Separating predisposition, precipitating events, and perpetuating factors helps explain persistence without blaming the person for trying to cope.

In the behavioral account of insomnia, the bed can become associated with effort, frustration, and alertness. A prediction such as “being awake means tomorrow is ruined” can intensify arousal and encourage further compensatory behavior. Cognitive work examines the prediction against experience and develops a more proportionate account of likely difficulty. It does not require pretending sleep loss has no consequences.

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, or CBT-I, addresses these learned associations and maintaining responses through structured treatment. Sleep hygiene concerns supportive conditions; it does not encompass the whole treatment. The reviewed clinician describes repeated visits, sleep diaries, and adjustments, with some procedures reflecting her own practice. Understanding that rationale is different from independently calculating a restricted sleep schedule. Persistent symptoms can also coexist with breathing disorders, medication effects, or other conditions, so a behavioral explanation should remain open to additional contributors.

**Source basis:** [D341](SOURCES.md#d341), E341-02 [00:08:27], E341-05 [00:25:07], E341-06 [00:27:50], E341-08 [00:34:33], E341-18 [01:06:53], and E341-28 [01:39:26].

**Check:** A person's stressful project ended, but bedtime now brings worry and prolonged wakefulness. Why might “the stressor is gone” fail to explain the current problem?

**Expected answer:** The trigger and maintaining factors can differ. Learned alertness, predictions, and coping responses may persist; identifying them does not exclude other contributors or prescribe a treatment schedule.

## HOS-DEP-SL-03 — Ask what a sleep treatment actually improves

**Purpose:** Distinguish a plausible mechanism, a measured sleep effect, and a benefit that matters to the person.

A sleep intervention can change several outcomes: time to fall asleep, remembered awakenings, total sleep, sleep stages, distress, or next-day functioning. These outcomes need not move together. An amnestic effect can reduce recall of wakefulness without a comparable improvement in sleep. Longer drug persistence might help cover the night while leaving unwanted morning sleepiness. A favorable change in one sleep stage does not by itself establish better long-term health.

Read a treatment claim in three steps. First, identify the problem it is meant to address, such as circadian timing or excessive arousal. Second, identify what was actually measured, over what duration and against which comparator. Third, ask whether the observed change improved a valued outcome at an acceptable burden. A persuasive mechanism helps explain a hypothesis; it cannot substitute for those later questions.

The reviewed orexin-antagonist discussion illustrates this distinction. A small, two-night human study reported changes in some cerebrospinal-fluid markers associated with Alzheimer disease, without improved measured sleep. That result does not establish prevention of dementia. Supplement studies introduce another issue: a result for a tested preparation may not apply to a differently formulated or inconsistently manufactured product. Keep mechanism, product identity, exposure, evidence, and the person's outcome distinct when interpreting apparent benefit.

**Source basis:** [D394](SOURCES.md#d394), E394-01 [00:02:04], E394-07 [00:15:09], E394-16 [00:33:12], E394-17 [00:34:13], and E394-28 [00:52:17].

**Check:** A product changes a brain-related biomarker over two nights, and someone reports remembering fewer awakenings. What would still be needed to claim that it improves long-term brain health?

**Expected answer:** Neither observation establishes lasting functional or disease benefit. Relevant longer-term clinical outcomes, an appropriate comparison, population and product details, and harms remain necessary; fewer remembered awakenings may not mean better sleep.
