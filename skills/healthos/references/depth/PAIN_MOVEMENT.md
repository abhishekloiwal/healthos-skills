# Pain, movement, and recovering function

**Purpose:** Understand pain and movement limitations without equating symptoms, tissue findings, and readiness for activity.

These are interpretation lessons; diagnosis, urgent symptoms, and treatment decisions remain governed by current clinical guidance and appropriate assessment.

## HOS-DEP-PM-01 — Describe the experience and its consequences separately

**Purpose:** Recognize pain as real while distinguishing its intensity from tissue findings and the activities it disrupts.

Pain is a sensory and emotional experience. Nociception is signaling related to potentially damaging stimuli; it is not identical to the experienced pain. Tissue findings, pain intensity, and interference with daily life therefore answer different questions. An image can show a structural change without explaining someone's current symptoms. Conversely, an unrevealing image does not establish that pain is imagined or that nothing relevant remains to investigate.

The nervous system processes bodily signals in context. Sleep, prior experience, fear, and expectations can influence that processing alongside peripheral tissue or nerve changes. Recognizing these influences does not assign blame or reduce pain to a psychological problem. Several contributors can operate together, and similar pain descriptions do not establish an identical mechanism in two people.

Consider two fictional people who report the same pain intensity. One still sleeps and works reasonably well; the other avoids stairs, loses sleep, and withdraws from friends. The number misses a substantial difference in burden. Useful description includes the pattern over time, activities affected, sleep, meaningful losses, and what the person fears the pain signifies. Improvement might mean greater participation or less fear even while sensation persists. These are legitimate outcomes alongside pain relief, without proving that a tissue lesion has healed or that new symptoms need no assessment.

**Source basis:** [D345](SOURCES.md#d345), E345-01 [00:03:58], E345-02 [00:10:37], E345-07 [00:35:05], E345-10 [00:54:50], E345-11 [01:03:31], E345-32 [02:30:52], and E345-33 [02:34:14]; [D287](SOURCES.md#d287), E287-01 [00:10:56].

**Check:** A person's pain score is unchanged, but they sleep better and have resumed valued social activities. Has nothing improved? Does an unrevealing scan mean the pain is unreal?

**Expected answer:** Function, sleep, and interference have improved despite unchanged reported intensity. The scan answers a limited structural question; it cannot invalidate the pain experience. Neither observation alone identifies the mechanism or establishes tissue recovery.

## HOS-DEP-PM-02 — Relief creates an opportunity, not proof of readiness

**Purpose:** Separate reduced symptoms from the capacity needed to resume a particular activity.

Symptoms can improve when a demand is removed or when a treatment changes pain processing. That improvement matters, but it does not by itself demonstrate restored strength, coordination, or tolerance for repeated loading. A painful tendon that becomes comfortable during rest has experienced a different demand from the same tendon during a long walk or a sporting activity. Rapid relief after a movement or treatment cannot establish equally rapid structural repair.

In an already assessed rehabilitation plan, distinguish three observations: symptoms at rest, response to the agreed activity, and response afterward. A person may feel comfortable during an activity yet notice symptoms the following morning. That pattern is information for reassessment, not a universal pain threshold or proof of a particular injury. The meaningful outcome is tolerating the relevant demands with a response judged acceptable within that person's plan.

Temporary support can change those demands. An orthotic, for example, may modify load without completing rehabilitation; structural differences can also justify continuing support. There is no universal deadline for removing it. Similarly, reducing one aggravating activity can preserve other appropriate activity while capacity is rebuilt. Ask what the current intervention enables, what remains untested, and who coordinates progression. Relief and rebuilding can complement each other, but one should not silently stand in for the other.

**Source basis:** [D296](SOURCES.md#d296), E296-04 [00:16:04], E296-08 [00:28:39], E296-15 [00:47:10], and E296-16 [00:49:36]; [D350](SOURCES.md#d350), E350-10 [00:29:59], E350-22 [01:07:29], E350-29 [01:36:05], and E350-30 [01:41:02].

**Check:** After resting, a runner's symptoms disappear. Their familiar full-distance run brings symptoms back the next morning. What did rest demonstrate, and what remains unresolved?

**Expected answer:** Symptoms settled under reduced demand. Readiness for the full running demand was not established, and delayed symptoms add relevant response information. The diagnosis, load progression, and appropriate next step require the existing assessment and care context; this is not permission to repeat the same challenge.

## HOS-DEP-PM-03 — Match control and movement to the task

**Purpose:** Understand why a recognizable limitation does not automatically imply the same exercise solution for everyone.

Mobility makes movement available; stability helps control movement and transfer force under the demands of a task. Neither means maximizing one property everywhere. A trunk may need movement during an ordinary reach and greater controlled stiffness when transferring a load. Increasing stiffness throughout the day is a different objective from developing enough control for a particular demand.

McGill explicitly rejects assigning his familiar stability routine to everyone: some people are already stiff, have another problem, or cannot comfortably use the required position. His useful principle is assessment before exercise selection. His rejection of the nonspecific-back-pain label is a source position; it does not prove that every unexplained complaint has an identifiable mechanical cause.

For example, two people may both find putting on shoes difficult. One reports that bending aggravates symptoms; another has limited comfortable reach even without pain. The shared task does not establish a shared cause or a need for more stretching or bracing. A descriptive record of activities that provoke, relieve, or leave symptoms unchanged can help a professional investigate the difference. It does not diagnose tissue damage from a movement pattern alone.

Choose outcomes tied to the person's task and review the response. Immediate improvement after coaching may reveal better use of existing capacity; it does not show that new muscle was built or that one routine is superior for everyone.

**Source basis:** [D287](SOURCES.md#d287), E287-02 [00:16:24], E287-14 [00:57:07], E287-20 [01:05:23], E287-21 [01:14:40], and E287-33 [02:21:09].

**Check:** Two people struggle with the same daily movement. Why is prescribing the same stretching or stability routine from that fact alone an unsupported step?

**Expected answer:** The task can be limited by different combinations of available motion, control, strength, symptoms, and context. An activity pattern can guide assessment but does not establish a diagnosis. Exercise selection and progression must follow the assessed need and response rather than the routine's familiarity.
